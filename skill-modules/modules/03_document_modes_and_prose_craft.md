# Module 03: Scientific prose

Use this guidance for scientific drafting and prose revision, including small edits. Write for strong scientists who may not know the subfield. Apply the shared principles to both grants and manuscripts, then consult the relevant document-specific subsection. For a local edit, use only the relevant checks; do not expand it into a full review.

Section organization is covered in Module 04. Submission requirements belong in Module 01, citation formats in Module 05, and layout and export in Module 07. Those tasks are not prerequisites for editing prose.

## 1. Shared scientific prose

### Sentence clarity and emphasis

- Begin with an established subject or information that connects to the preceding sentence. Give the reader enough context before introducing an unfamiliar concept or result.
- Put information deserving emphasis in a natural stress position, often the end of a clause or sentence. Match the sentence's emphasis to its scientific point. Do not force every finding to the end or manufacture punchlines.
- Make the entity the reader needs to follow the grammatical subject. It may be a biological entity, investigator, measurement, comparison, or previously introduced finding. Keep the subject and main verb close enough that qualifications do not obscure the action.
- Express actions through direct verbs when this improves clarity: "we measured blood pressure" rather than "measurement of blood pressure was accomplished." Prefer "evaluated" to "conducted an evaluation of." Retain precise scientific nouns when they are the clearest terms.
- Choose active or passive voice according to the intended focus. Active voice can clarify responsibility; passive voice can keep attention on the experimental subject or procedure. Neither is mandatory.
- Split sentences when their clauses compete for attention or the reader must hold too much unresolved information. Use sentence lengths suited to the content, without word-count targets or compulsory alternation between long and short sentences.

These are reader-oriented principles, not fixed algorithms. A deliberate departure is appropriate when it makes the meaning clearer.

### Paragraphs and the scientific argument

- Give each paragraph one main point or purpose. Supporting context, methods, evidence, and qualifications can belong together when they serve that point. Make the point clear early; a topic sentence is a useful default, not a required opening.
- Connect sentences through recognizable subjects and consistent terminology. Make pronouns and phrases such as "this effect" refer to an identifiable observation. Do not make readers reconstruct the connection between ideas.
- Connect paragraphs through the question, evidence, or reasoning that motivates the next step. Chronology is useful for explaining procedures, but a list of experiments is not a substitute for an argument. Do not invent a hypothesis or rationale to repair a transition.
- Where appropriate, move from context to the unresolved question, then to the work and its supported conclusion or expected contribution. Adapt this organization to the section's purpose rather than imposing a story formula on every paragraph.
- Open sections with the question, problem, or necessary context rather than a generic proclamation of importance. Use informative headings and sentence case unless the target requires otherwise.

### Scientific precision

Preserve facts, numbers, dates, citations, and technical meaning. Mark missing information with an actionable `[AUTHOR QUERY: ...]`; do not infer missing methods or generate new results during writing.

Use one correct term for each concept rather than rotating synonyms. Follow species-appropriate nomenclature, including HGNC, MGI, or RGD where relevant. Apply the correct gene and protein capitalization and italics, species binomials, SI units, and statistical notation. Integrate displayed equations into sentence grammar and punctuate them accordingly.

Preserve established acronyms during focused edits and assume they were defined elsewhere unless an acronym audit is requested. In new complete documents, define unfamiliar acronyms when required or needed for the audience. Avoid unnecessary or invented abbreviations without imposing fixed use counts or paragraph quotas. Use ordinary text unless the template or user requests boldface.

Use past tense for completed experimental actions and observations, present tense for established facts and broader interpretations, and future or conditional language for proposed work and anticipated outcomes. Choose tense by the statement's status, not simply by the document type.

Match claims to the design, comparison, intervention or exposure, outcome, and inferential assumptions. Description, association, prediction, causal effects, and mechanistic explanation serve different purposes; they are not a ladder of stronger wording. Predictive accuracy establishes neither causation nor mechanism. Randomized interventions and targeted perturbations can support causal claims but do not automatically establish specificity, necessity, sufficiency, or a mechanism. Ordinary observational reporting calls for associational language unless an explicit causal analysis and its assumptions justify more; do not introduce that analysis during editing.

Use "reduced" for a partial reduction, not "prevented." A partial reduction after inhibition may support a contribution to the response, but does not by itself establish necessity. Bound interpretations to tested conditions and controls. Report supplied statistical estimates and uncertainty accurately, and query unsupported claims or missing analysis details rather than silently strengthening the text. Module 02 provides reporting details when needed.

### Direct, non-promotional language

Write in a neutral, objective scientific tone. State concrete contributions and let evidence carry the argument. Remove vague significance claims, redundant qualifiers, conversational hooks, and formulaic transitions. Prefer "is," "are," and "has" when they are clearer than "serves as" or "stands as."

Avoid promotional terms such as "groundbreaking," "revolutionary," "unparalleled," "game-changing," "pivotal," "crucial," "valuable insights," "underscores," "highlights," "delve," "landscape," "tapestry," "fosters," and "harnesses." Use "robust" only with its technical meaning and claims of novelty only with supporting evidence. Do not force ideas into groups of three, cycle synonyms for variety, or create dramatic takeaway fragments. Keep headings and emphasis functional.

Use straight quotation marks in editable prose unless the target requires otherwise. Replace rhetorical em dashes and en dashes with ordinary punctuation or separate sentences. Preserve scientific ranges, minus signs, correctly hyphenated terms, and en-dash ligatures used as notation. Do not alter verbatim quotations, reference titles, code, identifiers, official templates, or other protected content to enforce style preferences.

## 2. Document-specific guidance

The shared standards above apply to both document types. Keep the differences below without duplicating their sentence-level rules.

### Grant proposals

- Explain why the question warrants study, how the proposed work addresses it, and what supports feasibility. Separate established evidence, completed preliminary work, proposed actions, and anticipated outcomes.
- State aims as objectives or questions, for example, "Aim 2: Determine whether receptor X regulates reversal learning." Do not present an anticipated finding as an established result.
- Describe expected contributions conditionally and discuss relevant uncertainty, risks, and alternatives. Follow the mechanism's expectations for preliminary evidence and milestones, as detailed in Module 04; a grant does not require a denser or more promotional prose style.

### Journal manuscripts

- Explain what was done, what was observed, and what the evidence supports. Keep observations distinct from broader interpretation and speculation.
- Results headings may state supported findings. Do not strengthen a claim to produce a more decisive heading.
- Keep the functions of Introduction, Methods, Results, and Discussion clear, including when the journal permits combined sections. Module 04 supplies section-specific guidance; the document need not follow a rigid narrative formula.

## 3. Revision checks

For substantial revisions, separate structure, argument, evidence, style, and copyediting checks. For small edits, apply only those needed:

1. Identify each paragraph's main point and check its connection to adjacent text.
2. Check that claims follow from the evidence and preserve supplied numbers, units, callouts, and citations. Query gaps instead of filling them.
3. Check sentence subjects, verbs, familiar-to-new flow, and placement of emphasis. Remove needless wording without losing necessary detail.
4. Check terminology, tense, grammar, punctuation, and artificial phrasing. Apply the requested scope and approvals; do not cut text solely to meet length limits.

## 4. Writing sources

The reader-expectation principles draw on Gopen and Swan and the related work of Williams and Bizup. Schimel informs the context-to-question-to-resolution organization; Alley informs clear, connected scientific exposition; Gastel and Day inform manuscript section functions. These are adaptations, not quotations or universal templates.

See [Writing-source notes](scientific_writing_sources.md) for verified source records, the material inspected, and verification limits. User preferences such as punctuation restrictions, acronym preservation during focused edits, and approval before length cuts are skill policies, not claims about those sources.
