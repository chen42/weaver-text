# Module 04: Section architecture and synthesis

**Applies to:** Grant proposals and journal manuscripts. Details the structural rhetoric, evidentiary requirements, and communicative function of each specific document section.

Apply the shared sentence and paragraph guidance in Module 03 to both document types. The separate sections below address their different purposes and required components.

## 1. Grant proposal sections

Use Heilmeier framing, quantitative milestones, or go/no-go gates when the mechanism requires them or the project warrants them, not as a universal grant proposal format.

### Specific Aims (or Project Objectives)

Use the mechanism's required title, structure, and length. A Specific Aims page is often limited to one page, but this is not a universal grant proposal requirement. The following structure is a drafting option, not a checklist for every proposal.
- **Opening from context to question**:
  1. Biological or clinical context: Define the problem with supported evidence and enough context for reviewers from adjacent fields.
  2. Knowledge gap or unmet need: Identify the specific unresolved question.
  3. Prior work: Explain what existing approaches establish and what remains unresolved, without assuming that they failed.
  4. Hypothesis or design premise: State the guiding hypothesis or objective and its evidentiary basis.
  5. Feasibility: Summarize relevant expertise, resources, and preliminary evidence without promising success.
- **Specific aims or objectives**:
  - Choose the number and level of detail to fit the project and mechanism, rather than a fixed aim count.
  - Use an objective or question in each title: "Aim 1: Determine whether phosphorylation of Thr34 decouples reversal plasticity..." Do not state an anticipated result as an established finding.
  - Describe the working hypothesis or design premise, experimental approach, and expected outputs.
  - Include quantitative milestones and go/no-go gates when required by the program or appropriate to the project; do not force them into every aim.
- **Concluding paragraph**:
  - State the expected deliverable and its specific contribution to the scientific question or program mission, conditional on successful completion.

### Significance and Background

- **Communicative function**: Explains why the problem matters and evaluates the evidence supporting the proposal.
- **Tense**: Present tense for established scientific facts; past tense for prior historical attempts and previous studies.
- **Structure**:
  - Problem framing: State the health, defense, or societal burden using figures from cited sources or supplied data. Use an author query when no supporting figure is available; do not estimate one.
  - Critical review of existing approaches: Describe documented limitations, such as dose-limiting off-target toxicity or insufficient biodistribution, without assuming that all previous strategies failed.
  - Mechanistic rationale: Connect the proposed target to the underlying biological pathway. Avoid vague significance claims.

### Innovation

- **Communicative function**: Identifies supported conceptual or technical differences from existing work. Use a separate Innovation section only when the mechanism calls for one.
- **Tense**: Present tense for established properties; future or conditional language for proposed capabilities and untested outcomes.
- **Structure**:
  - Conceptual innovation: Explain how the question or hypothesis differs from prior work where applicable. Do not assume that innovation must contradict consensus.
  - Technical innovation: Compare the platform, chemistry, or computational method with relevant existing approaches.
  - Comparative framing: State the supported difference and its expected benefit. Claims of priority, a resolved trade-off, or prior impossibility require specific evidence.

### Approach and Work Plan

- **Communicative function**: Presents the design, evidence of feasibility, and plans for managing uncertainty.
- **Tense**: Future tense for proposed actions; past tense for completed preliminary experiments.
- **Preliminary data expectations**: Calibrate the amount of preliminary evidence to the mechanism. Exploratory and early-stage mechanisms, such as an NIH R21 or a DARPA seedling award, do not require the extensive feasibility data expected for an R01 or a comparable multi-year project. Do not penalize a proposal for lacking preliminary data that its mechanism does not request.
- **Sub-aim architecture**: For each aim or sub-aim, detail:
  1. Rationale and working hypothesis.
  2. Experimental design: Biological models, negative and positive controls, vehicle treatments, and delivery protocols.
  3. Statistical rigor: Experimental and analysis units, sample size rationale, blinding, randomization, and planned analyses appropriate to the design. Report supplied power calculations where applicable; query missing calculations rather than generating them during writing.
  4. Expected outcomes: Explain how possible results address the question. Include quantitative success criteria when required or justified.
  5. Potential pitfalls and alternative strategies: Discuss plausible failure modes and feasible alternatives at the level appropriate to the mechanism and design.

## 2. Journal manuscript sections

### Overall narrative shape and functional boundaries

A useful default is to move from the biological context to the specific question, report the methods and observations, and then interpret the findings in context. Adapt this structure to the article type and journal; do not force every manuscript into the same narrative shape.

Keep section functions clear, including when the journal permits combined sections:
- The **Introduction** defines the question and rationale. Avoid extended method descriptions or a detailed results summary unless required by the article format.
- The **Methods** describe procedures, design decisions, and analyses sufficiently for evaluation and reproduction, without interpreting the study's findings.
- The **Results** present observations, quantitative evidence, and bounded interpretations needed to explain the findings. Reserve broader speculation and literature debates for the Discussion.
- The **Discussion** interprets findings, compares them with prior evidence, and evaluates limitations. It should not introduce unreported primary results.

### Title and Abstract

- **Title**: Specific, informative, and concise. State the primary finding or system. Avoid abbreviations, promotional adjectives, and punctuation such as semicolons or colons when prohibited by journal guidelines.
- **Abstract**: Use structured headings only when required or appropriate to the article type. The following content functions also apply to unstructured abstracts:
  - Background: State the central question or unresolved contradiction in 1 to 2 sentences.
  - Methods: Summarize the experimental model, primary intervention, and sample size.
  - Results: Provide quantitative findings with effect sizes, confidence intervals, or exact p-values. Every number must match the main text.
  - Conclusions: State the direct biological interpretation in 1 sentence. Do not overclaim.

### Introduction

- **Communicative function**: Guides the reader from a broad biological problem to the specific tested hypothesis.
- **Structure (The Inverted Pyramid)**:
  1. Broad context: The general biological phenomenon and its importance.
  2. Specific unknown: The critical gap in knowledge or unresolved controversy.
  3. Limitations of prior work: What existing studies establish and which documented limitations leave the question open, without assuming that they failed.
  4. Study statement: A concise 2 to 3 sentence closing that defines the specific question addressed and outlines the principal finding. Avoid "here we show" unless supported by the target journal style.

### Results

- **Communicative function**: Objective reporting of experimental observations without philosophical speculation.
- **Tense**: Past tense for the study's specific actions and observations ("treatment reduced," "we observed," "expression increased"). Present tense may describe a figure or a broader interpretation when appropriate.
- **Informative subsection headings**: Use supported findings rather than generic assay names when this improves orientation. Do not strengthen a claim to make a more decisive heading.
- **Paragraph organization**: Where useful, connect the question, observation, and bounded interpretation. Not every paragraph needs all three:
  1. Rationale and question: "To determine whether receptor X regulates downstream signaling Y..."
  2. Observation and quantitative data: "We treated isolated neurons with antagonist Z and observed a 45% reduction in phosphorylation ($F(1, 22) = 8.14$, $p = 0.009$, 95% CI [28%, 62%]; Figure 2B)..."
  3. Interim interpretation and transition: "These observations are consistent with a contribution of receptor X to acute phosphorylation in vitro. Because phosphorylation was transient, we next asked whether..." A partial reduction after antagonist treatment does not by itself establish receptor necessity or treatment specificity.
- **Reporting discipline**: Keep interpretations close to the measured outcomes and controls. Reserve speculative extrapolations for the Discussion.

### Discussion

- **Communicative function**: Interprets findings, reconciles contradictions with published literature, and acknowledges limitations.
- **Tense**: Present tense for interpretations; past tense when referring to the study's own data.
- **Structure**:
  1. Core finding restatement: Summarize the principal discovery in 1 to 2 sentences without repeating the Results text.
  2. Reconciliation with literature: Compare findings with prior studies. Detail whether differences stem from species, doses, timing, or assays.
  3. Mechanistic interpretation: When the author proposes a model, present it as an interpretation bounded by the reported data and cited evidence. Do not generate a model the author has not proposed; use an author query to ask whether one is intended.
  4. Transparent limitations: Mandatory discussion of design limits, unmeasured confounders, generalizability, and technical caveats.
  5. Concluding principle: Close on the most defensible, concrete conclusion.

### Materials and Methods

- **Communicative function**: Enables exact, independent experimental replication.
- **Tense**: Past tense. Passive or active voice chosen for clarity.
- **Rigor linkage**: Align every paragraph directly with the reporting standards in Module 02:
  - Sources, catalog numbers, and persistent identifiers for key materials as applicable. Include RRIDs where available or required; do not assume that every chemical or resource has an RRID.
  - Precise concentrations, buffer molarities, pH, and incubation times.
  - Centrifuge speeds reported in relative centrifugal force ($g$), not rpm.
  - Software names, versions, and repository links for all analysis code.
  - Detailed statistical models, including exact tests, post-hoc corrections, and handling of outliers.

## 3. Literature synthesis

Applies to Introductions, Discussions, and Grant Significance sections:
- **Systematic versus narrative synthesis**: Use the `honeybee-lit` skill for complex literature reviews, and follow the applicable PRISMA checklist when a systematic review or meta-analysis is in scope. For narrative literature searches, state the search boundaries and databases consulted, or point to the saved search record. A focused edit does not require a new search or persistent search log.
- **Primary source attribution**: Cite primary literature for specific empirical findings. Reserve review articles for broad conceptual overviews.
- **Differentiate consensus from controversy**: Explicitly distinguish established scientific consensus from disputed or conflicting findings.
- **Retraction and correction audit**: Check available publisher notices and bibliographic records for retractions, corrections, withdrawals, or expressions of concern. Record the source and date checked, and report incomplete checks rather than certifying the absence of notices.

## 4. Claim-to-evidence control

For substantive authoring, assess claim support within the requested scope. Maintain a persistent audit when requested or part of full production:
- Link central empirical claims to the relevant figure panel, table row, or cited passage. Verify that the evidence supports the claim, not merely that the reference metadata is valid.
- Clearly separate verified empirical observations from theoretical interpretations.
- Mark all missing methods, numbers, or parameters with `[AUTHOR QUERY: description of missing data]`. Never invent missing details.
- Label any proposed experiment, analysis, or control as planned future work until the user confirms execution.

## 5. Journal submission components

Identify required auxiliary components from the current journal instructions. The following are common components, not universal requirements:
- **Cover letter**: Include the title, article type, relevance to the journal, and required disclosures. Provide suggested or excluded reviewers only when requested or permitted.
- **Ethics statements**: Institutional approval bodies and reference protocol numbers for animal and human research.
- **Funding declaration**: Complete grant numbers and awarding agency names.
- **Author contributions (CRediT)**: Formal taxonomy of author roles (Conceptualization, Methodology, Software, Validation, Formal Analysis, Investigation, Resources, Data Curation, Writing - Original Draft, Writing - Review & Editing, Visualization, Supervision, Project Administration, Funding Acquisition).
- **Competing interest statement**: Explicit financial and non-financial declarations.
- **Acknowledgments**: Acknowledge technical assistance, core facilities, and non-author contributors with their documented permission.
